## Overview

This section tracks textile manufacturing through the lens of U.S. reindustrialization: which companies grow, spin, weave, dye, print, and sew fabric in the United States, which machines and chemicals they depend on, and which startups are changing the process. Non-U.S. companies are covered where they define a product category's supply chain — quilting fabric is the first example, because almost all of it is made overseas. Textile mills in the U.S. employed 78,300 people in August 2026 versus 503,300 in January 1990 (federal data cited by Forbes), so the starting point for most questions here is what is left and what is being rebuilt.

## Key Themes

- **Raw fiber leaves; value is added elsewhere.** The United States is projected to grow 13.6 million bales of cotton in 2026/27, export 12.3 million, and mill 1.6 million.
- **Quilting cotton is an Asia-made category with opaque sourcing.** The only brand-origin survey found (2017) points to Korea, Japan, Indonesia, Thailand, and India, with one U.S.-grown line; no major brand names its mills.
- **Machine building is European and Japanese.** Looms and spinning systems come from Belgium, Italy, Switzerland, Germany, and Japan; U.S. strength is in ginning and some dyeing equipment, and Continental Eagle announced in 2009 a move of gin-machinery production to India and ended U.S. production in 2012.
- **Dyes are concentrated.** Archroma (Switzerland) and DyStar (Singapore, Longsheng-owned) are the large dye houses named in sources; no large U.S.-headquartered textile-dye maker surfaced.
- **Reshoring is real but downstream and early.** Yarn, finishing, cut-and-sew, and software investments (Reshoring Textiles, Hamrick Mills partners, Anatar, Unspun) lead; new cotton-weaving capacity has not been documented.
