## Summary

Blue Origin, LLC is a Kent, Washington–based space company founded by Jeff Bezos in September 2000 that designs and operates a suborbital tourism/research vehicle (New Shepard), a heavy-lift orbital launch vehicle (New Glenn), lunar landers for NASA's Artemis program (Blue Moon), an in-space orbital tug (Blue Ring), and rocket engines sold to other launch providers (BE-4, on United Launch Alliance's Vulcan Centaur). New Glenn reached orbit on its first flight in January 2025 and achieved its first booster reuse in April 2026, but a May 2026 static-fire test destroyed a completed vehicle and heavily damaged its Cape Canaveral launch pad. In July 2026 the company raised $10 billion at a $130 billion valuation — its first outside capital in 26 years of being funded solely by Bezos — to rebuild the pad, ramp New Glenn production, and fund a newly announced satellite-broadband constellation (TeraWave) alongside its lunar and space-station programs. Blue Origin is directly linked to Firefly Aerospace, also documented in this section: Blue Origin subsidiary [Honeybee Robotics]({{< relref "../robotics/space-robotics/honeybee-robotics.md" >}}) is building the lunar rover for Firefly's Gruithuisen Domes mission, and Firefly co-founder Tom Markusic previously worked as a Blue Origin propulsion engineer.

## What It Is / How It Works

Blue Origin operates across four main product lines. New Shepard is a fully reusable suborbital rocket-and-capsule system used for short crewed and uncrewed research/tourism flights from Corn Ranch in West Texas; it has flown more than 40 missions since 2006. New Glenn is Blue Origin's orbital-class heavy-lift launch vehicle, powered by seven of the company's own BE-4 engines on the reusable first stage and two BE-3U engines on the expendable second stage (a "7×2" configuration); a larger "9×4" variant with roughly 55% more low-Earth-orbit payload capacity has been announced for a possible 2027 debut. The BE-4 engine is also sold to United Launch Alliance for its Vulcan Centaur rocket, making Blue Origin an engine supplier to a launch-vehicle competitor.

Blue Origin's lunar program, Blue Moon, has two variants: MK1 is an uncrewed cargo lander (the first unit, MK1-101 "Endurance," is undergoing environmental testing ahead of a targeted 2026 flight to the lunar south pole carrying NASA's SCALPSS camera payload) and MK2 is a larger, crewed variant developed under NASA's $3.4 billion Human Landing System contract awarded in May 2023, which calls for an uncrewed demonstration landing followed by a crewed landing on Artemis V (targeted 2029). Blue Ring is an orbital tug/servicing vehicle (payload capacity over 4,000 kg, delta-v over 3,000 m/s) whose first announced mission will carry space-domain-awareness sensors to geosynchronous orbit. In January 2026 Blue Origin unveiled TeraWave, a planned 5,408-satellite (5,280 low-Earth-orbit plus 128 medium-Earth-orbit) broadband constellation offering up to 6 Tbps of total network capacity and up to 144 Gbps per user, aimed at enterprise, data-center, and government customers, with deployment planned to begin in Q4 2027. Separately, Blue Origin has been developing the Orbital Reef commercial space station with Sierra Space, Boeing, and other partners under a NASA Commercial LEO Destinations agreement; the partnership was reported as strained in 2023 (only $24M of a $130M NASA award had been drawn), and this review found no confirmed, current public status update on the project.

Blue Origin acquired planetary-robotics firm [Honeybee Robotics]({{< relref "../robotics/space-robotics/honeybee-robotics.md" >}}) in a deal announced January 2022 — Honeybee now operates as a wholly owned Blue Origin subsidiary and is building the lunar rover for Firefly Aerospace's Gruithuisen Domes mission, a case (like Firefly's own supplier relationships) of a nominal launch-industry competitor supplying hardware to a peer.

## Notable Developments

- **2026-07-25/26:** Raised $10 billion at a $130 billion valuation in its first outside funding round in 26 years; Bezos contributed $2B, Coatue Management $4B, other investors $4B; proceeds earmarked for rebuilding Launch Complex 36, ramping New Glenn production toward ~100 annual launches, and funding TeraWave, Blue Moon, and Orbital Reef.
- **2026-05-30:** A completed New Glenn rocket was destroyed and Launch Complex 36's transporter-erector and a lightning tower were demolished in an explosion during a hot-fire (static-fire) engine test at Cape Canaveral; root cause undetermined at time of reporting; CEO Dave Limp pledged to resume flights before year-end.
- **2026-04-19:** NG-3 achieved New Glenn's first successful booster reuse (the NG-2 booster, "Never Tell Me The Odds"), but the AST SpaceMobile BlueBird 7 satellite payload was deployed to an incorrect orbit due to an off-nominal thermal condition affecting engine performance and was later de-orbited — a partial mission failure despite the successful reuse milestone.
- **2026-01-23/25:** Unveiled TeraWave, a planned 5,408-satellite broadband constellation (up to 6 Tbps capacity), targeting enterprise/government customers with deployment beginning Q4 2027.
- **2025-12:** Unveiled the New Glenn "9×4" variant (>70,000 kg to LEO) alongside 7×2 thrust upgrades, plus the Blue Ring orbital tug and a Deployable Aerobrake product line; disclosed the Blue Moon MK1-101 "Endurance" pathfinder lander targeting a lunar south pole landing.
- **2025-12:** Tory Bruno, former President & CEO of United Launch Alliance, joined as President, National Security, reporting to CEO Dave Limp, to lead Blue Origin's national-security/defense launch push.
- **2025-11-13:** NG-2 successfully launched NASA's twin ESCAPADE Mars probes and achieved New Glenn's first successful booster landing.
- **2025-02-13:** Laid off approximately 1,000 employees (~10% of the workforce) one month after NG-1; CEO Dave Limp told staff the company was not "set up for the kind of success that we really wanted to have."
- **2025-01-16:** NG-1 — New Glenn's first orbital flight, carrying the Blue Ring Pathfinder payload; reached orbit successfully, but the first-stage booster failed to land.
- **2023-09:** Jeff Bezos announced Amazon devices executive Dave Limp as Blue Origin's new CEO, succeeding founding CEO Bob Smith.
- **2023-05-19:** NASA awarded Blue Origin a $3.4 billion firm-fixed-price contract for the Blue Moon MK2 crewed lunar lander, targeting Artemis V.
- **2022-01/02:** Acquired planetary-robotics firm Honeybee Robotics, later selected by Firefly Aerospace to supply the rover for the Gruithuisen Domes mission.
- **2021-10:** Announced the Orbital Reef commercial space station partnership with Sierra Space, Boeing, and others; NASA awarded a Commercial LEO Destinations agreement in December 2021, and the team completed a System Definition Review in August 2022.
- **2020-02-17:** Opened its Huntsville, AL engine-production factory for BE-4/BE-3U manufacturing (~$200M investment).
- **2006-11-13:** Corn Ranch (Launch Site One) near Van Horn, TX became operational as Blue Origin's New Shepard launch site.
- **2000-09-08:** Founded by Jeff Bezos in Kent, WA.

### Claim: New Glenn achieved its "first successful booster reuse" on NG-3 (April 19, 2026)

**Status:** Verified for the reuse milestone itself; the mission as a whole was a partial failure, not a full success

**Supporting sources:**
- [Blue Origin — New Glenn's Third Mission (official)](https://www.blueorigin.com/news/new-glenn-ng-3-mission) — confirms the reused booster ("Never Tell Me The Odds") flew on both NG-2 and NG-3
- [List of New Glenn launches — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_New_Glenn_launches) — independently corroborates the reuse and notes the payload's incorrect orbit

**Refuting / questioning sources:** None dispute the reuse itself; however, sources agree the AST SpaceMobile BlueBird 7 payload reached the wrong orbit due to an off-nominal thermal condition and was subsequently de-orbited — a distinction between "booster reuse succeeded" and "mission succeeded" that parallels this section's general caution about parsing compound mission claims (see the Space Launch section's claim-verification guidance on partial vs. full success).

**Summary:** The reuse claim is accurate and independently corroborated, but should not be conflated with overall mission success — the payload did not achieve its intended orbit.
