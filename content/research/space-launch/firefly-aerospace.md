## Summary

Firefly Aerospace, Inc. (Nasdaq: FLY) is a Cedar Park, Texas–based space and defense technology company that designs, builds, and operates small-to-medium-lift orbital launch vehicles (Alpha, and Eclipse under co-development with Northrop Grumman), lunar landers (Blue Ghost), and in-space orbital transfer vehicles (Elytra). In March 2025 Firefly became the first commercial company in history to achieve a fully successful soft landing on the Moon, and the company completed one of the largest space-technology IPOs to date on August 7, 2025, closing its first trading day at roughly an $8.5 billion valuation. Firefly has also expanded into national-security sensing and software through its ~$855 million acquisition of missile-warning/ISR contractor SciTec (closed November 2025). The company's flagship Alpha rocket has a mixed flight record — including a September 2025 ground-test booster explosion — while Eclipse, a joint program with Northrop Grumman targeting NASA, national-security, and commercial medium-lift missions, has not yet flown as of this review.

## What It Is / How It Works

Firefly Aerospace positions itself as an "end-to-end responsive space" company spanning three product lines built on shared, flight-proven technologies: launch vehicles, lunar landers, and in-space orbital vehicles. Its Alpha rocket is a small-lift, carbon-composite launch vehicle powered by Firefly's own Reaver (first stage) and Lightning (second stage) engines, which use a patented tap-off cycle architecture; Alpha has flown seven times since September 2021 with a mixed record of full successes, partial failures, and one first-stage loss during ground testing (detailed below). Blue Ghost is Firefly's lunar lander line, built and operated under NASA's Commercial Lunar Payload Services (CLPS) program; Blue Ghost Mission 1 delivered ten NASA science and technology instruments to Mare Crisium in March 2025, becoming the first fully successful commercial soft landing on the Moon. Elytra is Firefly's family of orbital transfer vehicles — Dawn, Dusk, and Dark, in ascending order of maneuverability, power, and payload capacity — designed for on-demand payload delivery, space domain awareness, imaging, and long-haul communications from LEO to lunar orbit and beyond; Elytra Dark is also used as the "space tug" that inserts Blue Ghost and companion payloads into lunar orbit on multi-spacecraft missions.

Eclipse, formerly known as the Medium Launch Vehicle (MLV), is a co-development program with Northrop Grumman (which invested $50 million into the program in May 2025) intended to fill the gap between Alpha's small-lift capability and larger vehicles. Eclipse combines a first stage built on Firefly's Alpha propulsion and carbon-composite-structure technology (scaled up, using Miranda and Vira tap-off-cycle engines) with Northrop Grumman's Antares flight-proven avionics and a larger 5.4-meter payload fairing; the same first stage is also being developed for Northrop Grumman's own Antares 330 rocket. Eclipse is designed to deliver roughly 16,300 kg to low Earth orbit or 3,200 kg to geosynchronous transfer orbit, and Firefly has explicitly positioned it as a candidate for the US Space Force's National Security Space Launch (NSSL) Phase 3 Lane 1 program, whose contract ceiling was raised from $5.6 billion to $17 billion in July 2026. As of this review, Eclipse had not yet conducted a first flight; Firefly's CEO has cited a "late 2026" target, with the company's Alpha Block 2 upgrade separately pushed to Q4 2026.

Beyond its core launch/lander/orbital-vehicle business, Firefly has moved into national-security sensing and software: in October 2025 it announced the acquisition of SciTec, a Princeton, NJ–based missile-warning, ISR, and space-domain-awareness contractor, for approximately $855 million (deal closed November 5, 2025); see the [SciTec entry]({{< relref "../drone-detection/hardware/scitec.md" >}}) for detail on that business, which continues to operate as a Firefly subsidiary. Firefly's lunar rover for its NASA-awarded Gruithuisen Domes mission is supplied by Blue Origin subsidiary [Honeybee Robotics]({{< relref "../robotics/space-robotics/honeybee-robotics.md" >}}) — notable as a case of a nominal launch-industry competitor (Blue Origin) supplying hardware to Firefly. In 2026 Firefly also acquired Space-ng, a vision-navigation and autonomous-guidance software company, to strengthen the autonomy stack on Blue Ghost and Elytra, and announced a collaboration with NVIDIA using a Jetson edge-compute module to process imagery on-orbit for Firefly's Ocula lunar imaging service.

## Upcoming Missions

Per company and trade-press reporting as of this review, Firefly's publicly announced upcoming missions are:

- **Alpha Block II debut + second Block II mission (Q4 2026):** Stretched propellant tanks (2+ meters taller), a white thermal-protection coating, and enhanced performance; two flights planned in the quarter.
- **Eclipse first flight (target: 2027, slipped from an earlier "late 2026" target):** Medium-lift debut carrying over 16,000 kg to LEO; the Miranda first-stage engine has completed 150+ hot-fire tests and is moving toward formal qualification, and the first stage is designed for return-to-launch-site reuse, though early flights may defer booster-landing attempts to focus on reaching orbit.
- **Blue Ghost Mission 2 (target: NET 2027, per Firefly's own mission page — an earlier June 2025 company release had targeted 2026, so the schedule appears to have slipped):** Lands on the lunar far side near Nassau crater, a location shielded from Earth's radio noise. Carries six payloads from five nations: NASA's LuSEE-Night radio telescope, JPL's User Terminal, the European Space Agency's Lunar Pathfinder satellite, Fleet Space's SPIDER seismic instrument, the UAE's Rashid Rover 2, and Volta Space's LightPort wireless-power demonstration. Blue Ghost launches stacked atop the Elytra Dark orbital vehicle; Blue Ghost operates on the surface for at least 10 days while Elytra Dark remains in lunar orbit for five years as a communications/radio-calibration relay.
- **Ocula lunar imaging service (operations beginning with Blue Ghost Mission 2's Elytra Dark spacecraft):** A commercial lunar imaging service using Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory–developed telescopes capable of resolving ~8-inch features from 31 miles above the surface; after Blue Ghost's surface mission ends, Elytra Dark begins a five-year Ocula imaging campaign, with additional scope-equipped Elytra vehicles planned for 2028 and beyond.
- **NSSL Phase 3 Lane 1:** Firefly has positioned Eclipse as a candidate for this Space Force program (contract ceiling raised to $17B in July 2026), but this review found no confirmed Firefly task-order award under Lane 1 as of September 2026 — awards reported in 2026 trade coverage have gone to other providers (SpaceX, Rocket Lab, Stoke Space, Impulse Space, Relativity Space).
- **Sea-based launch capability:** Firefly, Lockheed Martin, and Seagate Space are jointly developing a sea-based launch option using Seagate's Gateway offshore platform, announced alongside the August 2026 Lockheed Martin contract extension.

## Employee Sentiment & Leadership History

### Leadership Turnover

Firefly has had four different CEOs in a little over four years — a pace of executive change worth tracking alongside its technical and financial progress:

- **Tom Markusic** (founder) stepped down as CEO on June 16, 2022, about three months after AE Industrial Partners completed a $75M Series B and took majority control of the company (March 2022); he moved to the board as Chief Technical Advisor (see Key People above). Both Markusic and AEI framed the change as a normal leadership transition for the company's next growth phase.
- **Bill Weber** succeeded Markusic and served as CEO until July 17, 2024, when he left immediately following a board investigation into an alleged inappropriate relationship with a Firefly employee; the company's public statement did not detail the circumstances.
- **Peter Schumacher** served as interim CEO after Weber's departure while the board searched for a permanent replacement.
- **Jason Kim** became CEO in October 2024 and remains in the role as of this review (see Key People above).

The COO seat has also turned over recently: **Dan Fermon** stepped down as COO effective December 6, 2025; **Ramon Sanchez** (25 years at Boeing, most recently Senior Director of Operations for Space, Intelligence & Weapons Systems) succeeded him effective December 22, 2025. In December 2025 Firefly's board also adopted a standardized executive severance plan for its named officers.

Separately, in December 2021 the Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States (CFIUS) requested that Ukrainian investor Max Polyakov's Noosphere Ventures divest its roughly 50% stake in Firefly over foreign-ownership national-security concerns; Firefly paused second-flight launch preparations and faced operational restrictions at Vandenberg Space Force Base during the divestment process. Polyakov sold his 58% stake for a symbolic $1 in February 2022; some restrictions on Polyakov and Noosphere were later eased in a 2024 review. This CFIUS history, combined with Firefly subsidiary SciTec's classified national-security and missile-warning work (see What It Is / How It Works above), is the most concrete, sourced context this review found for why Firefly's facilities may carry more visible physical security than a typical commercial launch startup — this review found no direct reporting confirming specific security measures at any one Firefly site, so treat this as informed context rather than a confirmed explanation.

### Employee & Workplace Sentiment

As of this review, Glassdoor lists Firefly at 3.3 out of 5 stars across 144 reviews, with 55% of reviewers saying they would recommend the company to a friend and 51% approving of the CEO; Indeed separately lists 3.3 out of 5 across 52 reviews.

**Commonly cited positives:** the mission itself ("incredible mission," meaningful aerospace work), benefits (health insurance, stock options, and vacation policies multiple reviewers described as generous), and colleagues ("genuinely great people"). Interns and early-career employees in particular highlight hands-on learning opportunities.

**Commonly cited negatives:** work-life balance (reviewers describe long hours and, in some cases, "terrible work life balance"), organizational silos and inter-department "blame games," and management quality — multiple reviewers describe upper and middle management (particularly at the director level) as inconsistent or dysfunctional, with specific complaints about nepotism in hiring/promotion decisions and high attrition among experienced staff. One 1-star review from a self-identified former manager (3+ years at the Cedar Park site) alleged leadership was "mercurial and incompetent" and described bullying incidents the reviewer said were resolved through financial settlements; this specific allegation could not be independently corroborated beyond the review itself, and Firefly has not publicly responded to it.

**Summary:** Sentiment in the available review sample is bimodal rather than uniformly positive or negative — reviewers who emphasize the mission, benefits, and colleagues tend to rate the company well overall, while reviewers focused on management, pace of work, and organizational structure tend to rate it poorly, and both patterns recur consistently across the sample.
