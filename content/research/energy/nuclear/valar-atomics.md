## Summary

Valar Atomics is an El Segundo, California microreactor developer founded in July 2023 by Isaiah Taylor. Its Ward 250 test reactor — a 100 kWt helium-cooled, graphite-moderated, TRISO-fuelled HTGR — achieved zero-power criticality on June 18, 2026 at the Utah San Rafael Energy Lab in Emery County, making it the **second** reactor to go critical under DOE's Reactor Pilot Program and the first DOE-authorized reactor operated outside the national laboratory system. Six weeks later the company raised a **$1B Series B led by Sequoia at a $6B valuation**, plus a $200M credit facility — an extraordinary valuation for a company whose reactor has produced, at most, single-digit kilowatts of electricity through a thermoelectric generator.

Valar is unusual on two axes. Commercially, it does not intend to sell reactors or primarily sell electricity: Taylor's stated position is that "electricity is a terrible product," and the model is to build "gigasites" of hundreds to thousands of units on a single licensed campus producing heat, hydrogen and **synthetic hydrocarbon fuels** via sulfur-iodine thermochemical water splitting. Regulatorily, Valar is not merely absent from the NRC docket — it is a co-plaintiff, with Last Energy and the states of Texas and Utah, in a federal suit arguing the NRC lacks statutory licensing authority over reactors below a defence-significance threshold.

It is also the most contested company in this section. Its power-output and "first to power AI infrastructure" claims are inconsistent across sources, a public radiation-dose claim by its founder was rebutted by roughly three orders of magnitude, and its investor roster and founder credentials have drawn sustained critical coverage. See Claim Verification.

## What It Is / How It Works

**A small gas reactor built for heat, not grid power.** Ward 250 is a helium-cooled, graphite-moderated HTGR using TRISO particle fuel — the same fuel form as [Kairos Power]({{< relref "kairos-power.md" >}}), [X-energy]({{< relref "x-energy.md" >}}), [Radiant Industries]({{< relref "radiant-industries.md" >}}) and [Antares Nuclear]({{< relref "antares-nuclear.md" >}}) elsewhere in this section, though in compact form rather than pebbles. Helium is chemically inert and permits very high outlet temperatures; the commercial design targets 950 °C+, which is the threshold that makes thermochemical hydrogen production and downstream synthetic fuel synthesis plausible. Ward 250 itself is a **test reactor** at 100 kWt design power — roughly one ten-thousandth of a commercial LWR — built to validate physics, fuel and controls, not to sell power.

**The "gigasite" and synthetic fuel thesis.** Valar's commercial argument departs sharply from every other company in this section. Rather than selling reactors to utilities or power to datacenters, it intends to own and operate very large numbers of small units on a single licensed site, converting heat directly into hydrogen and synthetic hydrocarbons via sulfur-iodine water splitting. Taylor's framing — "electricity is a terrible product" — is that a commodity electron sold into a grid captures far less value than a liquid fuel, and that the per-unit licensing overhead that makes microreactors uneconomic collapses if hundreds of units sit inside one licensing boundary. This is a genuinely different bet from the datacenter-PPA thesis driving [Oklo]({{< relref "oklo.md" >}}), [Aalo Atomics]({{< relref "aalo-atomics.md" >}}) and X-energy, and it is worth tracking on its own terms. It is also entirely unproven: no synthetic fuel has been produced, and the site licensing model the thesis depends on has no regulatory precedent.

**What criticality did and did not demonstrate.** DOE's own characterization is a *zero-power fuelled criticality demonstration*. The reactor sustained a chain reaction; it did not run at power, and no electricity was generated on June 18. Katy Huff, former DOE Assistant Secretary for Nuclear Energy, put the limitation bluntly: a zero-power criticality test "can be achieved without making real engineering progress on fuel or design; it simply checks if you can multiply neutrons." A subsequent July 2, 2026 stage demonstration ran the reactor at some fraction of power and routed hot helium through a thermoelectric generator to light up an Nvidia device — the specific claims around which are the most contested in this entry.

**Regulatory posture is a strategic position, not an oversight.** Valar operates under DOE authorization via Executive Order 14301, a pathway that NEI notes "explicitly eliminated the need for NRC expert design reviews." Simultaneously, Valar is suing the NRC to establish that microreactors fall outside its licensing authority under the Atomic Energy Act, and has stood up a Philippines subsidiary that ANS reports was chosen partly "to avoid U.S. regulatory burdens." Whatever the merits, the practical consequence is unchanged and worth stating plainly: **without NRC authorization Valar cannot sell power commercially in the United States**, and it has filed nothing.

## Claim Verification

### Claim: Ward 250's power output and the "first reactor to power AI infrastructure" claim
**Status:** Unresolved — four mutually inconsistent figures; headline claim is company-sourced only

**Supporting:** ANS, the most careful trade source, reports the reactor "achieved a 10 kWt output" after criticality. Valar's Series B post states the reactor "generated electricity to power an NVIDIA Blackwell" and calls it "the first and only time an advanced reactor has directly powered AI infrastructure."

**Refuting / questioning:** Tom's Hardware reports the reactor ran at "37% of its full power" while simultaneously stating it produced "100 kilowatts of thermal energy" — internally contradictory, since 100 kWt *is* the design full power. KSL and Deseret report "100 kilowatts of electricity," which is almost certainly a thermal-to-electric unit conflation: 100 kWt through a thermoelectric generator, at typical TEG efficiencies of a few percent, yields single-digit kilowatts electric at best. No independent measurement of electrical output has been published. Tom's Hardware describes the load as an Nvidia RTX/DGX Spark-class **desktop PC**; Valar describes it as "an NVIDIA Blackwell." TechCrunch's neutral phrasing — "provided a small amount of power to an Nvidia AI chip" — is the safest characterization.

**Summary:** The reactor reached criticality and, at a July 2026 demonstration, produced a small amount of electricity through a thermoelectric generator sufficient to run a desktop-class device. "Powered AI infrastructure" is marketing framing for a demo load, and every specific power number in circulation is either company-sourced, internally inconsistent, or a probable unit error. Do not cite a Ward 250 electrical output figure.

### Claim: "First company ever to take a nuclear reactor critical outside of a national lab"
**Status:** Overbroad as stated by the company

**Supporting:** DOE and NucNet do describe Ward 250 as a first, and the Utah site is genuinely not a national laboratory.

**Refuting / questioning:** DOE's own framing is narrower and specific: the first **DOE-authorized** reactor built and operated outside the national laboratory system. University research reactors have gone critical outside national labs in the United States for roughly seventy years.

**Summary:** Use DOE's formulation. The achievement is real and the framing is inflated.

### Claim: Total funding raised
**Status:** No source gives a clean lifetime figure

**Supporting:** Individual rounds are well documented: $1.5M pre-seed, $19M seed (Feb 2025), $130M Series A (Nov 2025), a reported $450M extension (Mar 2026, $2B valuation), $1B + $200M credit (Aug 2026).

**Refuting / questioning:** Valar's own post cites "$1.2 billion," which is the August event alone. NEI reported "~$580M raised" as of June 2026. Sacra reports ~$600.5M lifetime pre-Series B and separately mislabels the March 2026 round as the Sequoia-led Series B — contradicting both TechCrunch and Valar's own materials, which identify the August $1B as the Series B. Sacra also lists a 2024 founding year contradicted by three better sources.

**Summary:** Announced rounds sum to roughly $1.8B including debt (~$1.6B equity). State the individual rounds rather than a cumulative total, and treat Sacra as unreliable for this company.

### Claim: Commercial reactor power rating (5 MWe vs 80 MWe)
**Status:** Unresolved discrepancy

**Supporting:** NEI, NucNet and Canary Media all report the commercial variant as "scalable to 5 MWe" in 2026 coverage.

**Refuting / questioning:** Contrary's company profile states 80 MWe per unit. The two cannot be reconciled from published sources unless they describe different products or the 80 MWe figure is an older gigasite-aggregate spec.

**Summary:** Use 5 MWe with the caveat, and treat 80 MWe as an unverified or superseded figure.

### Claim: Ward 250 fuel enrichment
**Status:** Never stated

**Supporting:** Contrary describes Valar's fuel as LEU below 20% U-235; NEI describes the NOVA Core experiment as using HALEU TRISO.

**Refuting / questioning:** No source reviewed gives a numeric enrichment for Ward 250 itself. The distinction matters materially: HALEU dependency would place Valar in the same constrained fuel queue as Oklo and X-energy documented in this section's supply chain notes, while sub-20% LEU with in-house fabrication would not.

**Summary:** Unresolved. Do not assume either.

## Risk & Skepticism Notes

- **Zero-power criticality is a low technical bar.** Katy Huff (former DOE Assistant Secretary for Nuclear Energy) and Edwin Lyman (Union of Concerned Scientists, quoted on the parallel Antares milestone) both argue these demonstrations say nothing about safety or commercial viability. POWER additionally notes Valar "initially targeted July 4 **power operations**, not zero-power criticality" — a goalpost shift.
- **Regulatory bypass is the model, not an accident.** DOE authorization, a lawsuit against the NRC, and a Philippines subsidiary are three expressions of the same strategy. Nuclear Innovation Alliance warns the litigation, if successful, could produce "a patchwork of state-level oversight." Neutron Bytes reports DOE has separately loosened microreactor regulatory requirements. Elemental Energy's counter-argument is worth recording: "nuclear energy is safe because it has been strongly regulated — not because it's not hazardous."
- **Investor scrutiny.** Grist reported that Masha Bucher of Day One Ventures, which co-led the $130M Series A, worked as Jeffrey Epstein's publicist in 2017, and separately documented her Russian political ties; Grist reports Taylor maintained the relationship after those disclosures, unlike other founders. Recorded here because it has been raised repeatedly in coverage of the company, not as a technical judgement.
- **Valuation versus demonstrated output.** A $6B valuation rests on a 100 kWt test reactor that has run briefly at a fraction of power, no NRC pathway, no PPA, no named commercial customer, and no dated commercial deployment target. Canary Media notes SMR developers "still need to prove that the technology makes economic sense" and that Valar's political alignment "could become a liability whenever Democrats retake power."
- **No commercial timeline exists.** Unlike every other company in this section, Valar has published no date for a first commercial deployment.
